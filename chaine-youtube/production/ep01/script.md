## BLOC 1 — COLD OPEN · 0:00–0:25

> **Visuel** : noir une demi-seconde, puis un ordre de virement reconstitué qui remplit l'écran. Ken Burns lent vers le champ du montant. Pas de musique.

In the summer of two thousand twenty-three, the CEO of a small-town Kansas bank walked into his office and ordered a wire transfer.

[PAUSE]

Not his first. His eleventh.

[PAUSE]

By the time it cleared, he had sent forty-seven point one million dollars — almost everything the bank had — to people he had never met, in a place he couldn't name.

[PAUSE]

Three days later, the bank was gone. Fifty-four years old. One of only five American banks to fail that entire year.

## BLOC 2 — LE CONTRAT · 0:25–0:40

> **Visuel** : le mur d'enquête apparaît — onze ordres de virement épinglés en colonne. Le deuxième porte déjà un cercle rouge, hors focus. Nappe sonore discrète démarre.

The man who did it wasn't a hacker. He wasn't a gambler. He was the president of the Kansas Bankers Association's board — a man whose actual job was spotting scams like this one.

[PAUSE]

There was a moment — one specific moment — where this story could still have stopped. It's not where you think it is. It arrives at minute nine.

## BLOC 3 — LE REMBOBINAGE · 0:40–3:00

> **Visuel** : Elkhart, Kansas — Street View de la rue principale, carte du comté de Morton, photo de la façade de la banque (archives publiques). Chronologie en bas d'écran qui remonte vers « late 2022 ».

Elkhart, Kansas. Population: under two thousand. The kind of town where the bank isn't a building — it's a neighbor.

[PAUSE]

Heartland Tri-State Bank held the savings of farmers, ranchers, retirees. And its CEO, Shan Hanes, was exactly who you would want in that chair. Fifty-three years old. A church elder. A school board member. He had testified before Congress about protecting community banks.

[PAUSE]

In late two thousand twenty-two, Hanes got a message from someone he didn't know.

[PAUSE]

We don't know every word of that first conversation. What court documents tell us is what it led to: an investment. Cryptocurrency. A platform that showed his money growing — week after week, number after number.

[PAUSE]

Here's what Hanes could not see. The platform wasn't an investment losing or winning. It was a screen. A set. The kind of operation investigators call pig butchering — a term that describes exactly what it sounds like: you don't take the animal at once. You feed it first.

[PAUSE]

The numbers on his dashboard weren't returns. They were bait, painted to look like proof.

---

## BLOC 4 — LA MONTÉE · 3:00–9:00

> **Visuel** : alternance stricte document / dashboard reconstitué. Chaque paiement = une ligne de relevé qui se surligne. La chronologie avance en bas d'écran. Le rythme visuel s'accélère à mesure que les montants grossissent.

It started with his own money. That part is almost always true in these cases — the first losses are real losses, and they are the trap's most important ingredient.

[PAUSE]

Because when the platform told him he needed to deposit more to unlock his gains, walking away would have meant admitting the first money was gone.

[PAUSE]

So he didn't walk away.

[PAUSE]

In early two thousand twenty-three, forty thousand dollars disappeared from Elkhart Church of Christ — his church. The congregation he prayed with.

[PAUSE]

Then ten thousand from a local investment club. Small money, pooled by friends.

[PAUSE]

Then his daughter's college fund. Sixty thousand dollars.

[PAUSE]

Stop on that one for a second. Not because it's the biggest number in this story — it's nowhere close. But because of what it tells you about where he was. A man doesn't reach for his daughter's tuition because he's greedy. He reaches for it because he is certain — certain — that the money is coming back. The platform had spent months manufacturing exactly that certainty.

[PAUSE]

And then the personal money ran out. And the platform asked again.

[PAUSE]

What Hanes did next is the line he can never come back from. In May two thousand twenty-three, he initiated the first wire transfer out of Heartland Tri-State Bank's own funds. Not his money. The bank's.

[PAUSE]

Watch the dates. May. June. July. Eleven wires in roughly ten weeks. Each one bigger than caution, smaller than the promise. Forty-seven point one million dollars — moved from an FDIC-insured community bank to cryptocurrency wallets controlled by, in the Justice Department's words, unidentified third parties.

[PAUSE]

Eleven separate times, a man trained to detect fraud walked past every alarm he had spent his career installing.

[PAUSE]

And the platform? The platform kept telling him the same thing these operations always say: the money is right there. You can see it. It just needs one more deposit to unlock.

## BLOC 5 — LA BASCULE · 9:00–13:00

> **Visuel** : d'abord un plan fixe — la chronologie s'arrête. Puis les documents de la chute : couverture de presse locale, communiqué FDIC, communiqué DOJ. Le cercle rouge du deuxième virement revient dans le champ, toujours pas commenté.

The person who finally pulled the thread wasn't a regulator. It was a customer.

[PAUSE]

Brian Mitchell — a local businessman, a farmer, a client of the bank — was approached by Hanes about a loan. Something in the ask felt wrong. Mitchell said no. And he started asking questions.

[PAUSE]

The questions traveled. Within days, the wires were exposed, the board was confronted with what was left — and what was left was almost nothing.

[PAUSE]

On July twenty-eighth, two thousand twenty-three, regulators closed Heartland Tri-State Bank. The FDIC absorbed the full forty-seven point one million dollar loss. Depositors were protected. The bank's investors — many of them neighbors, many of them people Hanes had known his whole life — lost nine million dollars. Their shares went to zero.

[PAUSE]

The scammers were never publicly identified. As of the sentencing, not one of the forty-seven million had come back from them.

[PAUSE]

Hanes pleaded guilty to a single count: embezzlement by a bank officer. His lawyer told the court he had no criminal record, that he took responsibility — and that he never made a dime. All of it true. None of it enough.

[PAUSE]

On August nineteenth, two thousand twenty-four, a federal judge sentenced him to two hundred ninety-three months. Twenty-four years and five months.

[PAUSE]

The U.S. Attorney put it in six words: Hanes' greed knew no bounds. The FBI's special agent in charge said something colder, and more precise: his job — the bank's job — was to protect its customers and identify fraudulent scams. Not to participate in them.

## BLOC 6 — LE SIGNAL · 13:00–14:30

> **Visuel** : la musique coupe net. Retour au mur d'enquête. La colonne des onze virements. La caméra descend lentement du virement 1 au virement 2. Le cercle rouge se trace à l'écran, en temps réel. Silence complet jusqu'à la fin du tracé.

So where was the moment. Where could this have stopped.

[PAUSE]

Not the first message — nobody audits a stranger saying hello. Not even the first wire. Listen to how the sentencing documents frame it: a first transfer, alone, is a catastrophic error of judgment. A man in crisis, making one terrible call.

[PAUSE]

The moment is the second wire.

[PAUSE]

Because the second wire is the one that required him to look at what he'd already done — the missing church money, the empty college fund, the first hole in the bank's accounts — and instead of reporting it, cover it.

[PAUSE]

From wire number two onward, every transfer had a second job. It wasn't just chasing the money. It was hiding the wire before it.

[PAUSE]

That's the anatomy of this scam — of most scams this size. They don't need you to be greedy. They need you to be one payment deep. Because the person who has lost something is the easiest person in the world to sell a way to get it back.

[PAUSE]

Nine more wires followed the second one. The bank died on the eleventh.

[PAUSE]

Forty-seven million dollars, a church, a college fund, a town's trust — and the distance between one mistake and all of it was a single wire transfer that nobody saw.

[PAUSE]

That's the tell.
